* * *

Lately I’ve been trying to work out exactly how long I’ve been living in the Kingdom of Soissons.

The Kingdom of Soissons was not actually a kingdom, but by the time the smoke cleared everyone had already forgotten any other name for a group of people living together under common laws. The world was drawn in thicker lines now. Kings and subjects, heroes and feuds. What we know is that some time around 460 AD, a Roman aristocrat called Aegidius was appointed as magister militum per Gallias or commander of the legions in Gaul. This was not such a great post. Ten years earlier the province had been burst open by Attila and his Huns; now little Germanic kingdoms were wriggling around in the wound, sometimes nominally under Roman authority, sometimes fighting the Empire, sometimes fighting each other. Shortly afterwards the emperor who’d given Aegidius his post was deposed and assassinated by his magister militum in Italy. There were a few more emperors after that, little flickering nonentities like Olybrius and Glycerius, before the whole sad charade was wrapped up for good. In its place there was now something called the Kingdom of Italy, which was ruled by a man called Odoacer who was probably a Goth but maybe a Hun or possibly some kind of Turk. Beyond the Alps there were more barbarian kingdoms: the Burgundians, the Alemani, the Rugii. But in a distant swathe of what’s now northern France, from Nantes to Reims and the Loire to the Somme, Aegidus still held his post, governing the last province of an empire that no longer existed.

It lasted ten years. Not such a short time. One fiftieth the lifespan of the entire empire. Long enough to live in. Long enough for people to meet and marry and have children, and raise them in a world where _Romanitas_ still held. I imagine that life in Soissons was dominated by a kind of eerie calm. People playing dress-up, dressing up as themselves. Pretending the old world is still there, while heretics and idol-worshipping pagans mass on the borders. It could have held out longer. The Kingdom of Soissons was large, bigger than any of its barbarian neighbours. Its lands were fertile and well-watered. It was governed under a Roman bureaucracy, which was vastly more efficient than the spoils-sharing systems that surrounded it. It had the benefits of a thousand years of literacy and civilisation. But none of that mattered. Either history is on your side or it isn’t, and they could only hold out against history for so long.

Ten years after the fall of the Western Roman Empire, an idol-worshipping pagan called Hlodowig tore through the Roman armies at Soissons, destroyed the last Roman province in the west, and set up his own kingdom in the ruins. (We’re not even certain that Soissons was the administrative centre of the state; it’s just named after the battle in which it ended.) Aegidius might have thought he was preserving Roman civilisation from the end of the world, but whatever it is that rules history, God or Geist, had other plans. Twenty-two years after the Battle of Soissons, Hlodowig converted to Nicene Christianity. You might know him as Clovis I, King of the Franks. Three hundred years later, his successor Charlemagne would go to Rome, where Pope Leo III put a crown on his head and declared him Roman Emperor. In the end it wasn’t Soissons that inherited the old world, but the people that destroyed it.

There are Kingdoms of Soissons everywhere, things that have outlived themselves but keep limping on. Marriages: sometimes a couple will go to northern France for their honeymoon and never really leave, slogging through decade after loveless decade in grey Soissons, both fully aware that the thing has been over for a very long time, but still mysteriously unwilling to disengage. There’s still a Blockbuster Video in Bend, Oregon. You can still go to Kmart in Guam. If you still care even slightly about the fate of the literary novel in an age of screen-swiping imbeciles, you are, like me, a citizen of Soissons.

Thomas Pynchon’s _Vineland_ is set in the Kingdom of Soissons. It’s 1984, the year of Reagan’s re-election, sixteen years since the radical counterculture of the 1960s reached its climax and started to ebb away. All the radicals and revolutionaries are addicted to weed or TV now, living in the city of Vineland, a sanctuary for washed-up hippies where they can peacefully moulder away as the world around them grows plastic and cruel. ‘Dismantle the New Deal, reverse the effects of World War II, restore fascism at home and around the world.’ There’s the same sense in _Inherent Vice_. ‘At every gathering—concert, peace rally, love-in, be-in, and freak-in, here, up north, back east, wherever—those dark crews had been busy all along, reclaiming the music, the resistance to power, the sexual desire from epic to everyday, all they could sweep up, for the ancient forces of greed and fear.’ There was a beautiful moment when we might have been ruled by something other than greed and fear, but it’s gone. The best you can do is huddle in its wreckage.

Paul Thomas Anderson’s _One Battle After Another_, which is loosely based on _Vineland_, is also set in the Kingdom of Soissons. This time it’s the present. And we do seem to be in a repeat of the 1980s, another era of reactionary retrenchment. If anything, it’s worse. Things are bad out there. The plump, soft, rosy-cheeked boys who staff for mainstream Republican politicians don’t even read _Bronze Age Mindset_ any more, they just chortle away at Twitter accounts that post the words _total nigger death_ over and over again. This is normal now. State policy is informed by a kitchen cabinet of anime-watching paedophiles. Twelve-year-old kids are self-identifying as incels. Their only dreams for the future are to have eight hundred thousand dollars a year, the ugliest Miami apartment imaginable, and some kind of racial holocaust. No one can make eye contact any more. It’s getting bad.

And the film is a good image of this present, spookily good. The bulk of the story is about a military unit that invades the small Californian town of Baktan Cross, rounding up its population of druggies and migrants. This takes place so its commander can join a society of mawkish milkdrinkers called the Christmas Adventurers Club. It was filmed in early 2024: before the last US election, the accompanying vibe shift, the ICE goons unleashed on major American cities to conduct mass kidnap operations and fatly roll tear gas canisters at anyone who frowned at them on the street. But the images are exactly the same. This shouldn’t be surprising. For all the noise and spectacle of Trump’s immigration raids, he’s actually deported people at a substantially lower rate than either Obama or Biden. The noise and spectacle is the point, though; it’s all just grievance and revenge, making your enemies feel terror, acting out a theatre of terror on the streets of Chicago and LA, to be consumed in sixty-second increments by the Twitter-addled base. _One Battle After Another_ is not an _image_ that pre-emptied _reality_; Paul Thomas Anderson and Donald Trump are both great American artists with a deep instinct for the swirling content of their country’s id, and they both happened to land on the same scene.

Where I think the film stumbles is in its representation of the left. The real reason the soldiers are in Baktan Cross is to find a sixteen-year-old girl, who lives with her paranoid, addled father in a hideout in the woods. He’s a former revolutionary; he and the girl’s mother once belonged to an underground group called the French 75. But she was arrested after a bank robbery that went wrong, and gave up the rest of the group; now they’re all either dead or in hiding, and he’s reduced to rambling half-remembered Howard Zinn material to his daughter’s history teacher. It’s easy to sympathetically portray a burned-out former revolutionary. We’re all living in the wreckage of what we wanted to be when we were younger. Failure is relatable; it makes audiences feel seen. But what failure are we even talking about here?

In the film’s prologue, set sixteen years before the main story, we get to see the French 75 in their prime. Attacking military bases, robbing banks, putting bombs in courthouses, all leather jackets and Afros and carbines. If _One Battle After Another_ is set in the present day—and from the iPhones it seems to be—then the revolutionary prologue would have to take place in the year 2009. I remember 2009. The closest thing we had to a revolutionary black underground that year was when Barack Obama invited Henry Louis Gates for an awkward beer in the White House. All the stuff we see in the film is 60s shit. It really did happen, but not recently; we’re about as far away from the Black Panthers as they were from the reign of Queen Victoria. The difference between _One Battle After Another_ and _Vineland_ is that _One Battle_ is set in a post-revolutionary stupor without any actual revolutionary period preceding it. The last time the radical left was ascendant, leftist radicals weren’t bombing courthouses. They were trying to get a food writer fired for culturally appropriating the chickpea.

Critics don’t seem to really know what to make of Ari Aster’s _Eddington_. It’s set in 2020, it’s clearly _about_ 2020, but what is it _saying_? Not that the lockdowns and mask manadates were good, exactly, but it’s not straightforwardly saying they were bad either. Some of the characters scream about structural racism, and some of them scream about how the virus was invented in a lab by Satanic paedophiles: is the film trying to say that these are the _same thing_? Which side is it on? The problem is that film critics are journalists, and like all journalists they’re only capable of thinking of things in terms of what’s important. If something exists, it must signify; it needs to be relevant. They don’t get _Eddington_, because _Eddington_ is ultimately a film about how nothing that happened in 2020 actually mattered one bit.

The 2020 in _Eddington_ is a very foreign place. A lot of the humour in the film comes from just dispassionately presenting the kind of thing people routinely did in those days. It’s funny to see people standing in a long socially distanced line outside the supermarket, or with their nostrils pointlessly sticking out over their masks. From the standpoint of the present, these people make less sense than any medieval peasant or bone-jangling witch-doctor. The film is ethnographic, voyeuristic, peering into the world of these ooga-booga savages as they act out their madness. It observes, but it doesn’t explain. The fact that we all used to _be_ those ooga-booga savages, only five years ago, only sharpens the disjunction.

Sometimes you might wear an old coat for the first time in years and find a surgical mask crumpled up in the pocket. Sometimes you go to a newsagent that hasn’t taken down the paper sign by the register, reminding customers to stay six feet apart. This fragment of a lost world, like the feet of Ozymandias. God, it was all real. We really did briefly live in the weirdest society in the ethnographic record, where there was a taboo on anyone leaving their face uncovered, and none of the members of the tribe could come within an arm’s reach of each other in public. When the borders finally opened that year I went to New York: my friends told me I had to wear a mask in the street, even though we all knew by then that the risk of outdoor transmission was basically zero, because otherwise people might think I didn’t _like_ the masks, which was tantamount to being a Trump supporter. And meanwhile people really did keep saying things like the kids in _Eddington_, who suddenly start telling their parents at dinner that ‘right now I’m only white critical, but the goal is to be a white abolitionist, which means dismantling whiteness and not allowing whiteness to ressert itself.’ There was a year in which this kind of drivel was practically mandatory, and depending on your social circle, you could face serious consequences for not agreeing with it. You could be fired from your job for not believing that progress, politeness, and the written word are all aspects of white supremacy culture, which we must tear down to build a more equitable world. In _Eddington_, the kid who repeats this line ends up moving to Florida to become a vape-puffing right-wing influencer. That’s what happened to that.

But even within the mad atmosphere of the film, all these ideological conflicts are meaningless. The story is about a political struggle between a woke corporate Latino mayor and a sweet but psychotic right-wing conspiracist sheriff, who drives around in a police cruiser plastered with signs that say LETS FIX EACH OTHERS HEARTS and YOUR BEING MANIPULATED. But by the end, political victory comes with literal paralysis. It was all hollow and futile; it never mattered. Their struggle is a cipher for the real change coming to the small town of Eddington, New Mexico, which is the arrival of an enormous new AI data centre. None of the characters really know what this thing is. The conspiracists think it has something to do with deepfakes and, by association, the deep state. The libs think it’s good for jobs. But we know. This is where they’re training the large language models; it’s the embryonic form of our world, the world of 2025, buried in the impossibly foreign world of the past. In _Eddington_, the AI company is called Solidgoldmagikarp. ‘Solidgoldmagikarp’ is a glitch token, a character string that makes AIs produce strange and unsettling outputs, and which they’re mysteriously incapable of actually outputting themselves. It was first discovered in 2023. A maddening and unutterable thing, retrojected into the past. Like the conquistadors at the end of _Apocalypto_. Like finding a bunch of tweets about K-pop lovingly illuminated in a medieval manuscript. The data centre is a kind of anti-Soissons: not a splinter of the past still lingering in the present, but a small, sprouting fragment of the future.

Something has gone very badly wrong if the task of representing the future within the present has fallen to a silent, empty data centre in the middle of the desert. Once, that job belonged to _the left_. The real movement that abolishes the present state of things. The new, superior relations of production maturing within the framework of the old society. Muriel Rukeyser said socialists are exiles from the future. What happened? How long, exactly, have we been living in the Kingdom of Soissons?

2020 is a plausible enough date. That was the year the Sanders-Corbyn project finally collapsed for good after a second consecutive defeat for each figure; you could see the ideological frenzy that followed as a kind of supernova, all the waste energies left behind by millennial socialism burning themselves out. I had a pretty close experience of that collapse: in the weeks before the 2019 election I was spending all my free hours tramping around swing seats on the fringes of London, walking along the edge of the North Circular, great piles of wet leaves piled up between the wet 1930s semis, chipped paint on the pebbledash, flaking waterlogged doors. Usually, when I knocked, there’d be some hunched-over ancient inside, living in a home that smelled of gently decomposing cats, all dark and damp, curtains permanently closed. Could her local Labour candidate count on her vote? A flicker of terror. She wasn’t sure she could totter all the way to the polling station by herself. At this point, beaming with pride, I told that I could note down her address and, come Election Day, her local Labour Party would send a car round and give her a lift. The polls were not looking good, but I had the strange feeling that we just might pull this off. Jeremy Corbyn could become Prime Minister, propelled by the largest mass political party in Europe. We were the movement that cared; we cared enough to make sure that every voter, no matter how feeble and close to death, could exercise her democratic rights by voting for us. We believed that the freedom of each is the condition for the freedom of all. Our hearts were light and our vision was pure; we had a world to gain.

That was in December 2019. We then proceeded to eat shit in such a historic fashion, in a defeat so utterly humiliating, that roughly half my comrades from that era bounced right off the entire left-wing project in embarrassment and have now fallen into the bony clutches of the Church. At exactly the same time, people in a distant city in China started being hospitalised with a mysterious new respiratory infection.

Now it’s all died down again, the plague and the mania that followed it, that same style of left populism is starting to express itself again. Lingering scrag-end of millennial socialism. Six years on from his historic defeat, Jeremy Corbyn is once again at the front of a left-wing political party in Britain. Somehow, the mood is not the same.

In July, it was announced that he was forming a new party, which he would co-lead with Zarah Sultana, defecting from Labour. The problem was that the announcement didn’t come from Corbyn, who had apparently not yet agreed to anything of the sort, but Sultana. Once the two had agreed on the party’s existence and the godawful interim name Your Party, they started issuing separate portals for new members, on separate websites, collecting their fees in separate bank accounts. The Corbyn faction said Sultana was trying to carry out an internal coup. Sultana said Corbyn was part of a sexist boys’ club, and that a Parliamentary faction was trying to seize power from the membership before the party had even constituted itself. She wanted radical democratic measures. All policies decided on by the membership, all posts filled by sortition, mandatory reselection of MPs for every Parliament.

The actual substance of the dispute is murkier. Read around it and you’ll find a lot of very guarded references to social conservatism, whether the party should embrace social conservatives, or whether it should be accountable to the grassroots instead. What they’re actually talking about are the other four MPs attached to the party, aligned with the Corbyn faction: Shockat Adam, Adnan Hussain, Ayoub Khan, and Iqbal Mohamed. These were elected last year in protest against the Labour party’s general indifference to the genocide in Gaza, but aside from their position on Palestine they’re not really meaningfully on the left. Local businessmen, lawyers, community leaders. Shockat Adam has a chain of opticians. Adnan Hussain owns rental properties, which means Your Party has a higher proportion of private landlords in its ranks than any other party, including the Conservatives. They oppose any attacks on the welfare state, because the constituencies they represent include a lot of people who rely on state services, but what they really represent is a narrow, sectional, interest-group politics based on the preferences of British Muslims. Pro-Palestine, pro-private schools, pro-business. According to right-wing hysterics, this is the first stage in the Islamisation of British politics, but in the real world nothing is so clear-cut: the ‘Muslim’ faction is led by Jeremy Corbyn, a socialist, while the ‘socialist’ faction, which embraces trans rights and the materialist conception of history and so on, is led by Zarah Sultana, a Muslim.

What the split really represents is two paths to two very different kinds of power. The Sultana faction wants to use the grassroots party democracy to craft a platform that will secure the votes of the 5-15% of the country who strongly identify as ideological leftists, and be totally repellent to everyone else. The Corbyn faction wants to deploy the Palestinian cause to help quietly integrate local government into ethnic patronage networks. Choose wisely.

If, for some reason, neither of these options amaze you, there is another. Since the eco-populist Zack Polanski was elected leader in September this year, membership in the Green party has doubled to 130,000, and they’re now polling at 15%, about even with Labour. Not bad. Much of this is down to Polanski himself, who has an undeniably charismatic straight-talking manner and a set of bold left-wing policies. Most prominently, his grand centrepiece, the great revolutionary idea that’s been searing its way through the newspapers and whispered with horror on all the political panel shows, the thing on which he’s staked his career—a wealth tax. 1% on assets over £10 million. That’s it.

Hard to imagine a bigger failure of the political imagination. This country is in serious trouble. The capitalist classes have failed to properly allocate their capital: instead of going into productive industry, it’s all been parked in the property sector, inflating asset prices while the rest of the economy mouldered. Most job growth was in low-waged, low-skilled, low-capital intensity sectors; meanwhile the average house accrued more value than the average worker made in a year. But all this eventually caught up with them. The rentier model has hit its limits; asset prices are stagnating; corporate profits trending down; the rich are already fleeing the country. Talking about a wealth tax, at this point in the crisis, is just the instinctive thrashing about of a left-populism that no longer has any real answers. Trying to milk a dead cow. A rhetorical pose, the only thing we still know how to do.

Meanwhile, the growth in low-skilled and low-wage jobs has almost entirely been filled by people born abroad. Imported labour has a lot of advantages for the capitalist classes. As Karen and Barbara Fields pointed out, the reason planters in colonial Virginia imported slaves from Africa was that African labour ‘had not taken part in the long history of negotiation and contest in which the English lower classes had worked out the relationship between themselves and their superiors. Therefore, the custom and law that embodied that history did not apply to them.’ The same goes for the people who have crossed oceans and continents to sleep five to a room on matresses on the floor, so they can cycle around delivering your Gourmet Burger Kitchen order when you’re lazy to go to a restaurant. Today, after five years of unprecedented migration, Britain is turning into a racial caste society. There is a mostly-white middle class whose consumption is subsidised by the low cost of labour. There is a mostly-Asian service class doing broadly humiliating work for generally humiliating wages, sometimes topped up by the state through social housing and other benefits to prevent the cost of labour rising. Finally, there’s the former industrial working class, dwindling and disorientated, priced out of the cities, no longer necessary for anyone, but still numerous enough to express their generally incoherent fury at the ballot box and, increasingly, through racist violence. Does this situation seem good to you? It doesn’t seem good to me! Pro-migration politics are no longer anti-racist, they’re white supremacy by another name. Meanwhile, Zack Polanski’s response has been to wander around one of the slums where the modern-day slave labourers are housed, beaming about what a wonderful and diverse community it is, and reading out a poem for children by Benjamin Zephaniah.

What I’m starting to worry is that it’s always been like this. Leftists love to imagine that we’re the people with all the good ideas; the tragedy of the world is that no one ever listens to us, and our otherwise perfect political projects keep mysteriously failing. We also like to imagine that we’re the only people in the world motivated by kindness and empathy and a righteous anger against all forms of oppression. We believe that everything should be nice, and all the bad things should be made illegal. We’re awfully smug for a political tendency that’s administered some of the greatest catastrophes of the twentieth century, mass starvation, mass graves, industrial executions, pyramids of skulls. And for a movement whose main achievement this century has been absolutely nothing, we’re strangely smug about tactics too. Last weekend, seven million nice earnest geriatrics came out to protest against Trump’s authoritarianism in some deep state-backed, PAC-funded, TV news-promoted nonsense called No Kings, which also turned out to be the largest single-day protest in American history. For most of the left, the immediate response was to sneer. Ew, csoringe, libs, cringe, pottering around with their signs about how they’d rather be at brunch. Always brunch with these people. Big horsey grins, horsey laughs, chomping at avocadoes. Their tame, hokey chants. ‘We gonna heal the hate in this nation.’ ‘Love not hate makes America great.’ What are they even doing? What makes them think any of this is going to change anything at all?

And we’re right; the No Kings rallies are good exercise for people in their eighties but not much else. But what have we been doing? For the last two years, the organised left has poured essentially all of its energies into the Palestine solidarity movement. Like a lot of people, I’ve spent those two years taking a series of leisurely weekend strolls for Gaza. In London they’ve been having these mass strolls every weekend; sometimes there have been nearly half a million people strolling against the war. The war now looks like it’s come to an end. Was that us? Public opinion in the West has turned decisively against Israel, but that probably has more to do with all the dead kids than the people giving speeches to each other in a public square. As far as I can tell, what happened is that Israel tried to kill the Hamas negotiators they were supposedly hashing out a deal with by bombing a Qatari government complex in Doha. And that was it; they’d finally gone too far. You can bomb Lebanon, Yemen, Syria, Iraq, and Iran as much as you like, you can snuff out maybe two hundred thousand lives in Gaza, you can tear through the Middle East like a pitbull in a neonatal clinic, but Qatar is off limits. On goes the muzzle. Show’s over. The millions of people who took part in the international Palestine solidarity movement had absolutely nothing to do with it. It’s not clear if we did anything at all. All we did was walk, fifty-two times a year, for two years straight.

There’s nothing wrong with not achieving anything. Not achieving anything is the default state of any oppositional movement. But it’s strange that when, last weekend, seven million nice earnest geriatrics came out against Trump’s authoritarianism in the largest single-day protest in American history, the left thought we were in any kind of position to throw scorn. Ew, cringe, libs, cringe, pottering around with their hokey signs about how they’d rather be at brunch. What are they even doing? What makes them think any of this is going to change anything at all? And we’re right; the No Kings rallies are good exercise for people in their eighties but not much else. But that’s us! That’s what we’ve been doing, every weekend, fifty-two times a year for two years! Laugh at their tame, hokey chants. ‘We gonna heal the hate in this nation.’ ‘Love not hate makes America great.’ Babble, but at least they’re coherent and capable of staying on message. Meanwhile the Gaza solidarity movement can’t decide whether it wants an end to the war and the genocide, or to fully endorse the disastrously failed strategy of violent Palestinian resistance. As you might have noticed if you don’t have a small tree growing out of your brain, these two aims are contradictory. Seventy-seven years of armed resistance have done absolutely nothing but heap up more and more suffering on the Palestinians. But there’s a weird taboo within the movement on publicly opposing violence, even if you happen to personally disagree with it.
