There is a hidden entity that’s been trying to lure me to Dubai. These days I can’t go online without having to flick through endless ads for an exclusive selection of one, two and three bedroom properties in the prestigious al-Gahbah Waterfront. Blonde East European women grinning unconvincingly, skin like pleather, puffy cheeks, tight red skirt, great slabs of teeth: when you’re picking your address for your new life in Dubai, choose luxury, choose al-Gahbah Waterfront. Apparently other men get porn. Maybe it’s the same blonde East European women in their feeds too, maybe they adopt different forms to tempt you with your own shameful desires. I am midway through my thirties and plastic-titted strangers don’t do it for me like they used to, but I _have_ been jadedly nosing around Rightmove, end of terrace nice, ungh high ceilings, oh fuck yeah solid wood floors, and let’s see they want _how_ much? In fucking _Walthamstow_? Drooping, slackening, return to rest. Meanwhile the entire internet seems to be intoning with one cheerily insistent voice: YOU WANT TO MOVE TO DUBAI. YOU WANT TO BUY AN APARTMENT IN DUBAI. I’ve never once expressed any such interest, but if I didn’t want it, why would they be advertising it to me? Why would I be followed by jangly ukelele music and footage of a two hundred storey tesseract folding in on itself for gorgeous five-dimensional sunset views? Call to action: what’s your budget for property in Dubai? The options are all in dirhams, as if I’d have any idea how much that is. Check the exchange rate. Oh. It’s really not that much. You could have a mould-splattered carbon-monoxide hovel in London, above the vape shop where they fence your stolen phone, twelve-year-old kids having machete fights at the bus stop, marginal tax rate two hundred and thirty-four percent so they can pay off the waspi women for not checking their post—or you could move to Dubai, pay zero income tax, and have a swimming pool in your balcony at al-Gahbah Waterfront. Did you know that the United Arab Emirates never formally abolished slavery? There’s no taxes, they’ve got this chocolate with pistachio cream inside, and if you want they’ll let you own slaves.

This entity is so determined to show me images of Dubai, it’s even been showing me the sort of images you’re supposedly not allowed to see. Nishant lives in Dubai and has 296 followers on Instagram, but for reasons I can’t explain I’ve been getting regular updates on his life. He lives in a dormitory for migrant workers. A lot of his videos are filmed in a large, long room with two long benches where about a hundred skinny South Asian men stand, each bent over a small gas cooker, each frying their own chapati to eat with their own little pot of chana masala. No air conditioning in there; great sweaty patches on everyone’s sleeveless tops; fat drops of sweat dripping into the food. Later Nishant’s in his bedroom, which he shares with five other people, doing his laundry in a plastic bucket on the floor between the two stacks of bunk beds. Then he sleeps. He is not posting this as an exposé. He is not lodging some kind of protest against his living conditions. The captions all say Dubai ke ladke followed by lots of heart and money emojis. In some of his other videos he’s lip-syncing to Bollywood songs on a rooftop at dawn, with that hypodermic skyline all pink and glowing across the desert behind him. Nishant is from Arrah in Bihar, and I’ve [seen what it’s like there](https://samkriss.substack.com/p/numb-in-india-finale-the-fire-sermon). He’s bragging about his enviable life abroad.

But this is part of the appeal, the misery. What the Emirates represent is a way out of the Arab world’s long cold twentieth century. After all those failed experiments with Baathism, pan-Arabism, socialism, anti-imperialism, enlightenment, modernisation, dragging the region out of the historical rut it’s been in since the Mongol sack of Baghdad, back to the forefront of world civilisation, all those Levantine intellectuals, all those dashing fedayeen with their linen shirts open three buttons down, handsome 70s stache, Kalashnikov, cigarette—after all that, the Emirates found another way. Forget enlightenment; just embody all the most feverish Orientalist imaginings and build your new Arab modernity on that. The whole country is spun out of some diseased Frenchman’s odalisque. Sybaritic and cruel, liberality without liberalism, smell of camel shit and rosewater, a yellow-grinned procurer defiling some flawless nude with his big coarse hands. They pay Instagram models to fly there, let them drink hundred-year-old champagne, then once everyone’s done with the selfies they debase them in ways you can’t imagine. Fantastic minarets obviously, half a mile high; dream-palaces, indoor ski slope in the middle of the desert, artificial archipelago in the shape of the entire world; all the follies a mad sheikh needs. Bungee jumping, paragliding, jetskis. Top floor restaurant, wagyu steak covered in gold leaf, ten thousand slaves entombed in the concrete.

It worked. For the rest of the world, modernisation no longer means Westernisation. No one wants to be like the West, pompously dysfunctional liberal democracies where you need to spend a hundred million on planning documents before you can put in a catflap. Even the West doesn’t want to be like the West any more. The great global attractor is the UAE. Development is Dubaification: more sensuous, more childlike, more sinister. Life as a series of toys and gimmicks, the Emiraticore culture that’s now ascendant everywhere, matcha labubu sober rave Kaws Mr Beast Benson Boone. A new and unsettling class of commodities, that no longer try to obscure the social relations that undergird them. You enjoy the labubu precisely _because_ it’s annoying: this is one small way of guaranteeing that somewhere in the production process, someone has suffered. The Sultan drowning his concubines for fun. Everyone gets to worship Satan. That’s part of the deal too, baked in to the whole project. Vathek abjures the Prophet; Tamburlaine desecrates the Qur’an; the Orientalist fantasy always involves making war against Heaven. You get the sense that the emirs would rather have as little as possible to do with Islam. Hoping that if they build enough malls their population will quietly stop believing in God. Replace Him with Japanese food and Italian cars and Ukrainian girls. No more holy wars. No desperate flights across the desert. Your only reward is in this dunya, but the dunya can be a very nice place.

Meanwhile, two thousand miles away, they’re shooting, hacking, butchering men women and children until the bloodstains can be seen from space.

Until 1971, Qatar and the Emirates were both British protectorates. Sunbaked chiefdoms built on pearl-diving and piracy. The British were there to put a stop to the latter; aside from that they didn’t care. Protect the routes to India. Before the money started flowing the other way, the currency in the Gulf states was the Indian rupee. For a while before independence the idea was that Qatar and the Emirates would band together. These were two small, sparsely populated states, perched on the edge of a violent part of the world. Both had just lost their foreign protector; both also happened to be sitting on enormous pools of the black gunk that powers the entire planet. Union made sense. Instead they ended up pursuing two very different survival strategies.

The Emirates became the kingdom of pleasure, conquering the world through its desires. Qatar went for the other route. The _other_ response to the failure of Arab modernity was a return to political Islam. A kind of consolation prize. Maybe we don’t _want_ to catch up with the West, where all their daughters are whores; maybe we have something _better_. Maybe we have the dignity of an endless holy war against the pleasure principle. The Qataris, with their money and their media, managed to enmesh themselves with the mass Islamist parties, which gave them a vast proxy army of furiously pious rustics across the Muslim world. The two states might not have intended to become deadly rivals, but obviously these two strategies will end up clashing with each other. The border between the Emirates and Qatar passes through every human heart. Things have mostly remained calm in the Gulf; it all plays out in Africa. In Egypt, Qatari-backed Islamists were massacred in the streets in a UAE-backed military coup. Libya is split between the UAE’s Government of National Stability and Qatar’s Government of National Unity; the forces of unity and stability keep sporadically firing truck-mounted machine guns into villages up and down the country. In Somalia, the id is building a port and a military base in the breakaway region of Somaliland, while the superego tries to shore up the central government in Mogadishu. And now, there’s also Sudan.

For a long time, Sudan was in the Qatari sphere, under the forces of sexual repression. Then, in 2019, its brutal Islamist dictatorship collapsed after a mass democratic revolution. Maybe you can still hear that an Arab dictatorship has been overthrown in a mass democratic revolution without feeling an immense pit of dread open in your guts, but not me. Usually, this means all the hopeful young liberal urbanites are about to get gunned down by yellow-eyed warlords as naked force rises into the vacuum. Best case, the old entrenched interests manage to claw back control. Worst case, one set of entrenched interests fears that the other will, under the banner of democracy and national rebirth, break whatever stable compact existed under the old regime. Then you get a war. This is what happened in Sudan: the old Khartoumese merchant elite, represented by the Sudanese Army, facing off against their former allies among the camel-herding tribes of the hinterlands, represented by the Rapid Support Forces. Suddenly conscripts on meth are tumbling out of Soviet prop planes on a barrel-bombing run. The skies over the capital are full of screaming suicide drones. Human abattoirs in the countryside.

If you’re an Emirati politician, watching it all over a matcha latte on an artificial island shaped like your own face, this is obviously fantastic news. Of course you’re going to side with whatever faction is opposing the traditionally Qatari-aligned military. They’re now on the side of sexual desire. Send them guns, send them JAIS N35 armoured fighting vehicles, drones, whatever they need. And if they then end up committing what might be the single most brutal massacre of the entire twenty-first century, maybe that’s embarrassing. But it’s not like anyone really seems to care.

Last month, the Rapid Support Forces captured el-Fasher, the capital of North Darfur and the last city in the region still held by the Sudanese Army. The city had been under siege for over a year, and when it finally fell the RSF were brutal. At the Saudi Maternity Hospital, they killed everyone. They took videos of themselves strolling through the wards, executing patients in their beds. Across the city, thousands, maybe tens of thousands forced to dig their own graves. Women kidnapped, held for ransom, raped, shot. Over ten days in el-Fasher they might have killed more people than Israel managed over two full years in Gaza. There is a genocide taking place in Darfur. But the reaction in the rest of the world has been strangely muted. During the Gaza genocide the word people used a lot was _humanity_. Humanity is being slaughtered in Gaza; the way you respond is a test of your humanity. You hear less of that about Sudan. Occasionally there are token gestures. The RSF get guns from the Emiratis, and the Emiratis are chummy with Israel, therefore all these things are the same. One big convenient struggle, helpfully encompassing everything. Sometimes you hear the slogan: _Free Palestine, free Congo, free Sudan_. But Sudan is not under foreign military occupation. Sudan _is_ free, the dictatorship fell and now every faction is free to assert itself through violence; that’s the whole problem. Nobody really cares enough to even learn what’s actually going on. Why not?

There are plenty of readymade answers out there. There’s the racist explanation, which is that no one really cares about Africa, and the anti-racist explanation, which is that no one really cares about Africa. There’s the paranoid decolonial explanation, in which Palestinian suffering is considered more important because the people inflicting it are Zionist Jews, and the hysterical Zionist explanation, in which Palestinian suffering is considered more important because the people inflicting it are Zionist Jews. The problem with all these theories, though, is that people don’t just care less about the genocide in Darfur than the genocide in Gaza. They care less about the genocide in Darfur than the _exact same genocide in Darfur_.

Do you remember 2006? I don’t, not really. If I try to cast my mind back I’m mostly left with the nagging feeling that absolutely everyone is constantly having sex except me. I think at the time I badly wanted to just fast-forward through the next two years of my life, skip straight to the bit where I’d already failed all my GCSEs and had been forced to live in an open-air landfill, picking through the piles of filth for scraps of food and copper wire. But I do vaguely remember that you couldn’t go anywhere without hearing _Crazy_ by Gnarls Barkley, and you also couldn’t go anywhere without hearing about Darfur.

Back then the perpetrators were called the Janjaweed, and they were killing on behalf of the Sudanese government, but the Janjaweed are essentially just a previous name adopted by the RSF. In 2006, this was a major issue. There were regular mass protests in London, New York, Washington DC, Paris, Rome, and Berlin against the genocide in Darfur. Students set up tent camps on their quads, demanding the universities divest from any company doing business in Sudan. Schoolkids wore green wristbands as a badge of support for the cause. Activists bought full-page adverts in major newspapers showing the burned villages and the piles of bodies. There were two Olympic torch relays, the official one and a special protest one about Darfur. That period also saw the first war between Israel and Hamas after the withdrawal of direct occupation from Gaza. Airstrikes across the strip, hundreds dead. Nobody paid too much attention. George Clooney was personally visiting the refugee camps over the Sudan-Chad border. Mia Farrow was going on hunger strike.

How much did any of this frantic activity actually improve things for the people of Darfur? Nothing, of course. All the raised awareness in the world just meant the difference between dying horribly in the scrublands and dying horribly in the scrublands while a bunch of rich people on the other side of the world craned their necks to see your final pathetic spasm.

But they had plans. Twenty years ago, the Save Darfur activists wanted the United States, with or without the UN, to militarily intervene in Sudan. Boots on the ground, helicopters in the air, cornfed American boys fighting and dying for the Fur and Massalit. Three years into the Iraq War, impossible to ignore just how bad an idea it had been, and the libs had now decided that the only thing wrong with George W Bush’s plan was that he’d invaded the wrong Arab country. It was Sudan, you were meant to go for Sudan. Maybe they had a point; Sudan is a good place for American bombs. In 1998, US airstrikes hit a pharmaceutical factory in Khartoum, which happened to supply half the country’s medicines, for essentially no reason except that everyone in the world had just heard about the President getting his dick sucked. Do it again! Get the other half! Mad: they were mad.

What’s changed between the madness of 2006 and the madness of 2025? In that time, the kind of libs that once came out for the Save Darfur movement have been on an impressive ideological journey. They have learned phrases like _white saviour narrative_ and _neocolonialism_. This is probably for the best. Unfortunately, though, that last one seems to have mutated inside their brains. A lot of people now seriously claim to believe that every non-Western society is fundamentally peaceful, respectful, and egalitarian, living in harmony with nature and itself. When faced with any evidence that the other 90% of the world’s population are actually ordinary, fallible humans living in ordinary, contingent, repressive social structures, they have to find a way to blame this on the relatively brief period of European colonialism, which is the source of every bad thing to have ever happened in the world. As a result, well-educated people now know dramatically _less_ about the genuinely interesting histories of the formerly colonised nations than they did before. It’s still possible to talk about Gaza in this context, even if you have to strategically ignore the fact that most Israelis—and the most fanatically racist Israelis in particular—are entirely indigenous to the Middle East. What about Sudan?

The genocide in Darfur is being perpetrated by traditionally nomadic Baggara people, who form the bulk of the RSF, against settled farming peoples like the Fur and Massalit. (The Baggara consider themselves to be Arab, basically the same as Saudis or Palestinians, while the Fur and Massalit, who they physically resemble in every way, are black. It’s stupid, but no more stupid than whatever system of racial classification you believe in.) This is part of the vast bloody war that’s being fought in a continuous band across the Sahel, as the climate heats up and the desert expands. The more crap you buy from Amazon, the bigger the Africa-swallowing abyss gets, the more farmers and herders are squeezed into the same narrow strip of desiccating land. That land cannot be put to two uses at once: herders need lots of wide open pasturage; farmers need to intensively plant every single acre. The herders are usually the more aggressive, but they’re also desperate. Herding land is already marginal, close to the desert. If they can’t seize new pastures from the farmers their way of life is over; they’ll have to sit around in slums receiving charity from NGOs. Not much of a life. But because farming is so much more productive than transhumance, the farmers tend to have much denser populations. The only way herders can overcome this imbalance is the way they’ve always overcome it: through brutal, gratuitous violence.

No one actually comes out and says any of this, though; they all pretend to have different reasons. In Mali the pastoralists claim to be fighting for the principle of ijtihad and against the distortions of the various schools of Islamic jurisprudence; the farmers say they’re fighting for national sovereignty, a multipolar world order, and Russia as a counterweight to Western imperialism. In northern Nigeria, the pastoralists are incensed by secular education and the lie that the Earth is a globe; the farmers are defending the legacy of the Sokoto Caliphate. In Sudan, the herders are the last line of defence against political Islam, which is why they keep calling the internationally recognised Sudanese government _terrorists_, while the farmers are fighting for their lives. In the twenty-first century this war has to wear a lot of masks, but it’s all one war, the greatest and oldest war in human history, Abel the shepherd of flocks against Cain the tiller of the soil, Amorite against Sumerian, Scythian against Greek, Xiongnu against Han, Mongol against everyone. The farmer-herder conflict is the only true constant throughout all of human history. Everything else that’s happened—the rise and fall of civilisations, the bloat and ebb of the European colonial empires, the first man on the Moon—is a minor sideshow. But you have no position on it at all. The screeching moralistic theory of international relations can’t help you here. You have no allegiance to either the farmers or the herders. Theoretically you know better, but in practice you think food just sort of appears in the supermarket overnight, swelling into its plastic packaging.

So Sudan is passed over in a deeply uncomfortable silence. The absolute most you can do is blame the Emiratis. From what I’ve seen, more people seem to be appalled at the UAE for its frankly marginal role in arming the RSF than at the RSF itself. This is the approved way of understanding any inscrutably indigenous foreign conflict: you just worm out any third-party involvement and then act like you’ve solved the whole thing.

The problem with this kind of analysis, though, is that it assumes people’s opinions about a foreign conflict are actually about that foreign conflict. In fact, absolutely nothing is ever really about itself. A lot of things really have changed in the last twenty years, but educated liberals’ attitudes to Africa are the least of them.

What was the deep psychic structure behind liberal interventionism? When people wore green wristbands or went on hunger strike for Darfur, what were they doing? What you’re looking at is essentially a kind of hysteria: they want to _be something_ to the other, the one who can save people, make things better, improve society. During the democratic era, these were the stakes of politics; it was all about getting other people to recognise you. You wanted to be loved and esteemed by other people in the world, so you would make a show of caring about the vulnerable and the suffering, even if this didn’t actually do anything to help them one bit. But as ways of securing recognition go, this isn’t really that bad; Hegelians will remember that the need for recognition traditionally manifests itself in history as a series of various forms of ugly subjugation. The struggles of that era were many, but they’re also over. We’re in the post-democratic era now, in which other people no longer exist.

The real world is still there, but it’s been reduced to a sort of vague colourful trim around the edges of your phone. Instead of people, you encounter horrible phrases that emerge from nowhere on your phone screen. You don’t want these creatures to recognise you as a self-consciousness; you just want to make them _go away_. We’ve finally picked off the scab that was ten thousand years of human history; we’re right back to the ‘vanishing moment’ of the original Hegelian encounter, in which the other appears as nothing except the negation of the self. This negation takes a lot of forms, but one of them is the figure of the political enemy: the laughing, inhuman blob that delegitimises whatever concepts you’ve used to build your self-identity. The political enemy wants to misgender you, or destroy your national community with mass immigration, or mock your religion; whatever it is, the existence of your political enemy is unbearable. You have constant intrusive daydreams about slitting their throats and painting slurs on your naked chest in their blood.

You’ll never actually _do_ it. You might have psychologically regressed to a primordial state, but you’re still a coward, living in a safe liberal society. You’ll just keep fantasising, and supporting deranged political movements in an attempt to annoy the horrible demons who live in your phone. Force them, at long last, into a seething, impotent silence. The other thing you have is Gaza. It doesn’t always map perfectly, but in general, whoever your political enemies are, they will support the opposite side to you in the conflict. There is a magical land in the southern Levant where the violence in your head is real, where your guys and their guys are constantly carrying out brutal massacres against each other. You get to greedily gobble up war footage and imagine it’s you. That’s _you_ heroically chucking a pipe bomb at your uncle’s Merkava tank. That’s _you_ heroically circling a refugee camp with your quadcopter drone, gunning down every nephew that moves.

Trying to stop the genocide in Sudan doesn’t do anything to upset your enemies. Pointedly asking why the people who marched for Gaza don’t care about Sudan _might_, so you see a lot of that. But if you wanted to, it would be very easy to make people care about Darfur again. All it would take is a loud, vocal contingent of RSF apologists in the Western media. They wouldn’t have to be good at their jobs; they’d just need to be incredibly annoying. Every time anyone said anything about the piles of bodies in el-Fasher, these people would have to yowl and cringe and try to shield their faces with two limp, soft, flabby hands. Aaah! What are you really saying here? Why is your heart filled with so much hate? You want to kill me, don’t you, you want to kill my beautiful family, all because I’m brave enough to stand up to the Burhan clique and their reign of terror… They’ll be right, you really will want to kill them. But all you’ll actually do is develop a misguided, uninformed, but suddenly very intense support for the Sudanese Armed Forces.

There are any number of names you can give this phenomenon, but I’ve been calling it matcha labubu politics. As human beings retreat from one another, everything becomes not just cruel and instrumental, but lurid, pointless, gimmicky, and _annoying_. Everyone’s sole remaining purpose, after all, is to annoy. From now on, every genocide will have to be a matcha labubu genocide, or else it won’t register. In Sudan, the actual military involvement of the actual United Arab Emirates is one thing, but there’s also the fact that you have built a Dubai for yourself inside your own soul. Maybe you will hear it calling out to you through means you can’t yet understand. Maybe you will hear it calling you home.
