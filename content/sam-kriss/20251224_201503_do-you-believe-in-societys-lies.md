Infoterrorists have sabotaged the Santa Claus Parade in Brantford, Ontario. Thirty thousand people line Dalhousie Street to watch Santa go by in his mechanical reindeer sleigh, sponsored by GrandBridge Energy. Adorable little kids, cheeks red against the cold, eyes coal-black with wonder, standing on tiptoes for a glimpse of the great Christmas magus. Santa, _the_ Santa, great potency, ancient king, who judges the world from his icy seat at the lifeless pole, and who will, for no clearly discernible reason, shortly be delivering them a Bluey Supermarket Play Set with fifteen pieces, including an exclusive Bingo figure—he’s here. He’s coming here, touching earth in this extremely medium-sized city in the flat cornlands of peninsular Ontario. A miracle. Magic beyond words. Instead, something terrible happened. Along the parade route, someone had left a series of terrible, obscene messages. SANTA IS FAKE, SANTA ISN’T REAL, YOUR PARENTS ARE SANTA, and YOUR FAMILY BUYS YOUR PRESENTS. The parade nearly tipped over into riot. Hundreds of parents trying to tear down the offending messages before they polluted the children. Fistfights, small fires. Hundreds of calls to the police. Two people mistakenly thought to be the perpetrators were very nearly lynched. Nobody is allowed to interfere with our right to nakedly lie to small children.

You can understand why they were so angry. This particular lie is vast, and it depends on a total omertà among all the conspirators. It’s been going for decades, centuries even, and the corruption goes all the way to the top. In 1961, a girl called Michelle wrote to President Kennedy, asking about Santa Claus. He wrote back: ‘You must not worry about Santa Claus. I talked with him yesterday and he is fine. He will be making his rounds again this Christmas.’ Michelle wrote to JFK because she assumed that surely the President of the United States could be trusted to tell the truth to an American citizen, and he _lied through his teeth_. The media is in on it too. In 1897, another brave young truthseeker wrote in to the New York _Sun_ to try to understand the world. ‘Papa says if you see it in the _Sun_ it’s so. Please tell me the truth: is there a Santa Claus?’ How did they reply? They lied. ‘Yes, Virginia, there is a Santa Claus.’ Newspapers reprint that response every year, drumming in the falsehood by sheer force of repetition. Corporations: every consumer-facing firm on earth will lie to their consumers that Santa is real. Obviously, the military-industrial complex is also involved. Every year, NORAD—the North American Aerospace Defence Command—pretends to track Santa’s movements on his reindeer sleigh across the world. Radar arrays swivelling, THAAD interceptors prepared to blow Santa out the sky at a moment’s notice. All that machinery, all those enormous defence budgets, all those kickbacks, to track an airborne object that _doesn’t even exist_…

But it doesn’t stop there. The tentacles of this thing reach into every corner of society. An architecture of deception so intricate it borders on absurdity. You can literally _meet_ the man, sit on his lap at a shopping centre or your local library, tell him exactly what you want for Christmas—but it’s not him. There’s a global network of impostors. They even have special institutions, like the Charles W Howard Santa Claus School in Midland, Michigan, where they train these operatives in the arts of deceit. Your teachers, whose job it is to tell you the truth about the world: every single one of them is deliberately, knowingly lying to you. Even _your parents_ are in on it. The world you think you inhabit is not the real world.

Some questions. Why do they do this? What does this conspiracy achieve? Why is it more wonderful and magical for children to believe that their gifts don’t come from their parents, who love them, but some stranger in the North Pole? We’ll get there. Most urgently, though: if they can lie about Santa, what else can they lie about? What is Santa Claus, if not a case zero for the propaganda model of society?

Like most people, when I was around twelve years old I suddenly discovered that society was made of lies. Everyone else inhabited the make-believe world sold to them by the capitalist media, but I could see through it; I knew the truths you’re not supposed to know. Advertisers really just want you to buy their products! Schools are really just there to give you skills for the workplace! Society is actually ruled by the government! This is probably a necessary step in the road towards political maturity; eventually you grow up and notice that practically everyone in society claims to be the only person brave enough to tell the truth in a world of lies. It’s weird: the more people there are insisting that the truth is being systematically covered up, the unlikelier it seems. It doesn’t help that most of the clandestine truth on offer is either obvious to the point of uselessness (‘Did you know racism is actually bad?’) or straightforwardly evil and incorrect (‘Did you know racism is actually good?’). So you end up concluding that while there might be different interpretations of how the world works, all the facts are, more or less, known. But then, sometimes, something happens.

Six years ago, something happened to Jeffrey Epstein in a prison cell in New York. I found it hard to reconcile with the basically sensible Marxist materialism I was using to understand the world. I thought social reality was essentially composed of surplus labour and the declining rate of profit, but Epstein seemed to suggest that there are things happening in the shadows that I had never imagined. In a way, it was like discovering, as a grown adult, that no, Santa Claus really is real. Santa’s implicit role is to be the guardian of the secret of the commodity-form. He hides the knowledge that all the wonderful presents are actually just a collection of exchange-values. The primordial forces of the world are fundamentally indifferent to little children, who tend not to actively participate in the labour or commodity markets. (Did you think it was a coincidence that Santa is a big guy with a big white beard, known for his impressive drinking abilities, and associated with the colour red?) Epstein’s secret is the opposite. Whoever it was pulling his strings, they were happy to let this failed maths teacher play around with their billions, pretending to be some kind of genius investor. Money is just a toy. Beyond a certain level it doesn’t really matter at all. The world really runs on stranger stuff. Magic wishes, sacrifices, the circuits of desire. Spooky flash photos of Bill Clinton and Donald Trump sharing verrucas in a hot tub. Somehow, this has to do with the fate of nations. War and revolution. Cities bombed into rubble in the lands of Armageddon. And children are not incidental; they’re at the ugly centre of the whole thing.

Is it possible for an entire social structure to be based on lies? Is it possible for a society to pretend to be something completely different to what it actually is? The ethnographic record says yes. In fact, it’s the norm.

The modern industrialised world is a frustrating place to live in. The more we extend our technical mastery over nature, the more anxious it makes us. Is this really it? Just minerals and sediments, physical processes, lumps of matter in the void? Is there nothing else? People in large swathes of rural West Africa didn’t have to wonder about this sort of thing: they knew. There really is a spiritual world, and it’s unbelievably powerful; what’s more, they’d seen it with their own eyes.

Outside the village, every village, the wild bush is inhabited by teeming spirits. They have different names: the Kono called them _nyomou_; the Gbandi called them _gafùi_; the Bambara called them _jinèw_; the Baoulé called them _bonu amuin_. Mostly these spirits stay in the woods, radiating powerful curses, but sometimes the wall between human life and wild nature cracks just a little, and the spirits come pouring in. First you’d hear their voices. Unearthly sounds: voiceless groans, low gurgling throb. Most people would rush inside the moment they heard the spirits groaning on the edge of the village. They had the power to kill anyone who saw them, but a few brave individuals would still peek through the eaves and the doorways of their houses. Spirits dancing in a frenzy through the suddenly empty streets. Lumpy creatures made of reeds and grasses, bits of the vegetable world come to life, but their thick figures were topped with horrible faces. Sometimes animal-headed, crocodilian, full of awful teeth. Or devil-like, with dozens of twisting horns. Or, worst of all, grotesque parodies of the human form. Eyes and a mouth, but garbled beyond reason. The spirits carried spears and clubs. Any human being they found would be slaughtered on the spot.

A lot of ordinary life in West Africa involved getting on the right side of these spirits. Disease happened when people upset the spirits: offer them the right sacrifices and they can also cure. Sometimes they’d curse the village with drought, and you had to give them whatever food you had left so they’d bring the rains again. The spirits knew how to make powerful medicines, which could guarantee success in hunting or make the young warriors invincible in combat. They knew the deep, old secrets of the earth.

Except, of course, they didn’t. The inhuman groans of the spirits were really made by horns and bullroarers. The spirits themselves were just people in costumes and masks. Specifically, the most wealthy and powerful people in the village, dressing up as supernatural monsters to terrify the rest of the population into submission. A lot of work went into maintaining this illusion. The elaborate masks were produced in secret bush workshops outside the village; if anyone saw them being made, he would either be hastily recruited into the conspiracy, or killed and eaten. Anyone who publicly doubted the reality of the spirits would usually become one of their victims. Conspirators weren’t safe either. If any one of them accidentally let his mask slip during a performance, he would also die. But you do have to wonder why they bothered. These people were already wealthy and powerful. These societies didn’t have systems of individual or transferable land ownership: being wealthy and powerful meant having a senior position in the kinship group, receiving lots of bridewealth, having a large retinue of pawns and slaves. Their public authority was already well-established. So why bother with this elaborate game of make-believe, in which the power doesn’t really belong to them at all, but to terrifying nonhuman forces from the edge of the visible world?

Something similar happened in what’s now Southern California, but this being SoCal it mostly revolved around astrology. In the traditional cosmology of the Chumash people, the universe is under constant threat from vast and hazy forces that are perpetually on the verge of obliterating all human society with floods, wildfires, and earthquakes. (Again, the beliefs of the Malibu area have not really changed.) The only thing standing against these forces were the Antap, a powerful hereditary caste of obsessive astronomer-chiefs.

The Antap are old, maybe one of the oldest institutions in human history. Like the forest-spirits of West Africa, they used bullroarers to generate the voices of the cosmic beings, along with bone whistles. Caches of bullroarers and whistles have been found in caves near Santa Barbara, dated to around 1500 BC. For more than three thousand years, they meticulously tracked the stars, and systematically dominated the rest of society. They held an enormous body of esoteric knowledge about the sky: the names of the stars and their meanings, the secrets of the zodiac and the phases of the moon. Some of this knowledge was encoded in the gorgeous rock art that filled the sacred caves of the Simi Hills. Commoners were forbidden from learning any of it. Their job was to pay constant taxes to the Antap in return for the rituals that kept the universe in order. The Antap could impose death sentences on essentially anyone they wanted: as another Californian also knew, sometimes, to prevent an earthquake, people have to die. It was also death for anyone to enter the _siliyik_, or sacred observatory, that stood in the middle of a Chumash village. In their tobacco-induced trances, members of the Antap claimed they could travel into the sky and talk to the stars, sometimes convince them to change their course. The most important festival was Kakunupmawa, or the winter solstice, a time of genuine mass terror, widespread frenzy and desperation. Over the course of the Christmas period, the Antap had to commune with the spirit of Old Man Sun and convince him to stop sinking lower and lower in the sky each day. If they fail, Old Man Sun will vanish below the horizon forever, and the world will die. But Old Man Sun is a corpse-eater. A grotesque naked man carrying a bark torch. As he moves across the sky every day, he collects the bones of the dead and devours them. The only way to stop him abandoning the world is to promise him bones.

Practically every human society features some similar kind of doubling. Political power can never just announce itself openly; it always has to pretend to be something else. Spirits of the forest, a war in the sky. What about in our own society? Are democracy, legitimacy, and the rule of law just more diffuse versions of the _nyomou_? We know it happens in at least one instance, because Santa Claus is an obvious instance of the same thing. In the traditional performance, once a year the paterfamilias, the _actual_ locus of power within the family, has to dress up as an alien entity. Reindeer-man, lord of the taiga; another old man traversing the sky at solstice-time, a great all-seeing solar eye, with the power to reward or punish at will. He forces his way into the house at night. Leave small offerings of milk and cookies for him, but don’t let him see you. A glimpse, a peek, from behind the sofa or through the banisters: that’s all.

Still, a lot of anthropologists don’t like concluding that the African masked dancers or the Californian astronomer-chiefs were simply _lying_. The Marxist-inflected account, in which these rituals are conceived of as part of a class struggle within a society, spreading false consciousness on behalf of a cynical and rapacious elite—these days, it’s very much out of fashion. Theories of false consciousness feel icky, presumptive; diminishing cherished cultural practices to the status of a dress-up game or a cheap magic trick. Colonial arrogance. These are collective acts of meaning-making. Who are we to say that the terrifying spirits are _really_ just men in masks? Are we so sure we know what’s real?

Luckily, another perspective is available. Quesalid was a witch-doctor who lived on Vancouver Island some time in the late nineteenth century. (His real name was Maxulagilis; Quesalid was the name he took after being initiated. White men knew him as George Hunt.) Unlike most witch-doctors, he wrote an autobiography. It begins: ‘I desired to learn about the shaman, whether it is true or whether it is made up and whether they pretend to be shamans.’ It turns out that indigenous people are also capable of scepticism.

Despite being an outsider, and a known doubter, Quesalid is initiated into some of the secret rites of the Nakwaxdax bands. ‘The fainting, the trembling of the body, always at night, the singing of two sacred songs for healing the sick, the singing of two sacred songs for trying to catch the soul of one who is nearly dead.’ He also learns the most powerful ritual in their repertoire. The Nakwaxdax witch-doctors claimed to be able to suck the sickness out of a human body: the patient would take off their clothes, and the witch-doctor would suck frenziedly on their skin. Then, when they pulled themselves upright again, they’d spit out a glob of bloodied matter into the palm of their hands. A wet crawling worm of disease. This is it, they’d say, this is the evil spirit that was inside you. Then they wrap the demon in cedar bark and bury it in hot ashes. This was a consistently successful medicine. Quesalid is taught how the performance really works. The witch-doctor would keep the down-feather of an eagle hidden underneath his upper lip; just before going in to suck on the patient, he’d bite down hard on the end of his own tongue. The bloody glob of matter is just a feather soaked in his own blood. Quesalid now knows for certain that the secret powers of the most respected people in his society are really just a trick. ‘This,’ he writes, ‘is the reason why I do not believe in all the doings of the shamans of all the tribes.’

But then, Quesalid discovers some other witch-doctors. Among the Koskimo, they also claim to be able to suck out the evil from a sick person’s body, but they don’t know the eagle-down trick. They just spit a gob of ordinary saliva into their hands and declare the patient cured. ‘Now I have at last found your great sickness, this rotten material. And so I have come and obtained this matter.’ When Quesalid performs the version he’s learned from the Nakwaxdax, the Koskimo are astounded, but their witch-doctors are furious. Not least because Quesalid’s patients also seem to recover much quicker than theirs do. Quesalid ends up being challenged to a series of magical contests, all of which he wins handily. He becomes known as a great and powerful sorcerer, maybe the most powerful on the island.

Finally, another witch-doctor comes to him in a panic, begging to be initiated into his secrets. ‘It won’t be bad what we say to each other, friend, but I only wish you to try to save my life for me, so that I may not die of shame, for I am a plaything of our people on account of what you did last night. I pray you to have mercy and tell me what stuck on the palm of your hand last night. Was it the true sickness or was it only made up? Pity me, friend.’ Quesalid doesn’t tell him. Instead, he starts demanding the other witch-doctor explain all of his own cures. Are _those_ real, or are they made up? His rival fesses immediately. ‘I tell a lie when I say that the alleged sickness which is put in my head ring of red cedar bark goes and bites into the post.’ Instead, there’s a thin nail hidden inside the bark. ‘I only press the nail into the post.’ He also has a raven rattle, which seems to hang suspended in the air. ‘That head of the raven is just tucked between my fingers. All these fools believe that it is truly biting my hand.’ He’d become a witch-doctor a long time ago, after pretending to faint on the beach. He lay there on the grey sands for hours while ravens circled overhead, and afterwards he told his friends that he’d entered a trance. The ravens had all turned into men, and they’d given him the secrets of the universe. But in fact, he admits, he was really just ‘covetous for the property of the sick men.’

Strangely, this experience doesn’t confirm Quesalid’s suspicion that the witch-doctors are all liars. Instead, he starts to gently believe in the art. Maybe what he does isn’t real, exactly, but it’s still _more_ real than the pathetic cures of his rivals. When he spits his own blood into his hand, people really do seem to get better. Who knows how these things really work? And there might be others who are even more genuine than him. Quesalid mentions another witch-doctor, who refuses to take payment from the people he treats. Maybe, he considers, this one’s magic is real.

Claude Lévi-Strauss riffs on this story in _The Magician and His Magic_. In a society like Quesalid’s, disease is not just an individual matter; it affects everyone. The presence of sick person is a symbolic rupture; it means the universe is out of balance. And because this rupture has its origin in the spirit world, everything that comes from the spirit world—which includes all thoughts, dreams, and knowledge—is out of order. Quesalid believed that his patients were usually sick because of something in psychic, dreaming life, and if his fake cures helped them it was because the patient ‘believed strongly in his dream about me.’ But the fake cure is a _real_ cure on the level of the social. ‘Because of their complementary disorders, the sorcerer-patient dyad incarnates for the group, in vivid and concrete fashion, an antagonism that is inherent in all thought but that normally remains vague and imprecise. The patient is all passivity and self-alienation, just as inexpressibility is the disease of the mind. The sorcerer is activity and self-projection, just as affectivity is the source of symbolism. The cure interrelates these opposite poles, facilitating the transition from one to the other, and demonstrates, within a total experience, the coherence of the psychic universe.’ In other words, the witch-doctor’s cures work precisely _because they are a lie_, because lies and fabrications are a necessary part of a healthy mental world. He takes the general malaise hanging over the society and _gives it shape_; he invents it into a glob of blood and slime. This foreign object can then be safely destroyed in the fire, and the society overcomes the division between hysteric and fantasist that’s inherent in all thought, until the next outbreak of disease.

Lévi-Strauss speculates that the same thing might happen in industrial society; in fact, it might happen to psychoanalysis itself. ‘The distressing trend which, for several years, has tended to transform the psychoanalytic system from a body of scientific hypotheses into a kind of diffuse mythology interpenetrating the consciousness of the group, could rapidly bring about a parallelism. When this happens, the value of the system will no longer be based upon real cures from which certain individuals can benefit, but on the sense of security that the group receives from the myth underlying the cure.’ This has absolutely happened. Freudianism might have waned as a mass idiom for the psyche, but its pop-psychological replacement works in precisely this way. For a lot of people, the cure for all potentially destabilising social contradictions is to identify an offending party, and tell them to go to therapy. ‘Therapy,’ in this world, is not a series of techniques for thinking through the events of individual life. It’s a magical rite, in which the red glob of badness that makes people behave poorly to one another is removed.

In fact, a lot of the most glaring examples of untruth in our society can be read this way. Two years ago, for instance, academics at the Museum of London published a paper arguing that structural racism had led to higher mortality rates for black Londoners during the Black Death of 1348. They had examined 145 skeletons from three London cemeteries, and found that 18.4% of the plague burials, but only 8.3% of non-plague burials, had recent African ancestry. They arrived at these figures by measuring the skulls. (Specifically, nasal and orbital bones.) At no point in their research did these people appear to consider that maybe, if their results were suggesting that somewhere between eight and eighteen percent of London was black _in the fourteenth century_, something might be wrong with their methodology. (Black people make up 13.5% of London _today_.) This obviously false result was then reported as fact in the BBC, the _Guardian_, and various other outlets known for occasionally raising the alarm about the need to combat fake news and misinformation. (The BBC article was edited to include some material acknowledging that race is a social construct, but apparently not so much of a social construct that you can’t discover it by measuring the width of someone’s nose.) Did the people who wrote this research, and the people who peer-reviewed it, and the people who reported on it in the press, _know_ that they were spreading falsehoods? Wrong way of looking at it. They were participating in a collective mythic fiction that helped shore up the integrity of the social whole. It definitely didn’t help that most of the people willing to publicly dispute these results happened to be some variety of slimy swivel-eyed racist. You don’t need to believe it in a literal sense. But you believe in the structure, the Antap of reputable authorities and peer-reviewed journals, enough to play along.

The liars are only play-acting. And most of the time, the people they’re supposedly deceiving are only playing along too. Even in the societies where forest-spirits suddenly pour out of the wilderness to slaughter people at random in the streets, a lot of the population are actually generally indifferent to the spirit world. Ethnographers have consistently found that most people in small hunting or farming societies are basically ignorant of their own people’s religious beliefs, even the ones that are public. A lot of the time, individuals cobble together their own idiosyncratic doctrines from whatever they find lying around. While doing his fieldwork with the Bushong of the western Congo, Jan Vansina encountered ‘three very independent thinkers’ among their elders. ‘One old man had come to the conclusion that there was no reality, that all experience is a shifting illusion. The second had developed a numerological type of metaphysics, and the last had evolved a cosmological scheme of great complexity which no one understood but himself.’ According to Brian Heyden, the secret societies that performed the deadly masked-spirit dances in West Africa often had no real esoteric knowledge at all. Everyone knew the rituals for appeasing the forest spirits, everyone knew how to make powerful medicines, except for around one tenth of the population, who were atheists and materialists and didn’t believe in any of it at all. If they still took part in the busy calendar of ritual events planned by the secret societies, it was with a kind of implicit condescension. Of course we know it’s not real, but we pretend to be terrified anyway; it’s a tradition, and anyway they did put so much effort into those masks…

And Santa too. The real secret of Christmas is that the children were never deluded. They know; they’ve always known; they were born knowing. Sometimes they don’t _realise_ that they know, but this is why the parents get so upset at signs like the ones in Brantford, Ontario. It’s why some people still think the worst thing Donald Trump ever did to a child was ask one if she still believed in Santa Claus, ‘because at seven it’s marginal, right?’ There’s no point at which Santa and your parents are _competing theories_ for the origin of presents; as soon as someone openly speaks the hidden knowledge in front of the children, the masquerade is over. They’re free, now, to say what they’ve always implicitly understood. A lot of the anti-Santa activists imagine that children might feel upset or betrayed that the adults in their lives have lied to them, or that the experience might make them less likely to receive other important social lessons. (Outside the literal-minded analytic-philosophy types who simply think lying is wrong, a lot of these people are evangelical Christians. If we tell our children false stories about Santa, they reason, and then reveal the deception, where does that leave Jesus?) But in fact, this almost never happens. When the deception ends, what most children feel is pride. They are initiates now. They have seen into the mysteries of the adult world. A first taste of the secrets of sex and death.

Santa Claus is the first and the most basic form of political propaganda we ever encounter. But what this means is that political propaganda is a game. The hidden truth of the world, the thing the all-powerful _they_ don’t want you know, the thing _they_ will kill to keep hidden: you already know it. Everyone does. You won’t say it, but you’ve known it as long as you’ve lived.
